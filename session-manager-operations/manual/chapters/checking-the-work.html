Trust, but verify — how the work gets checked

An agent can sound completely sure of itself and still be wrong. This chapter shows you the checks your agency runs before it calls anything done — and the one check that's still yours to make.

Why an agent can be confidently wrong

An agent writes its answer one word at a time, each word chosen because it fits well after the last one. Nothing in that process checks the answer against reality as it goes. A wrong answer can come out reading just as smooth and certain as a right one.

This isn't a flaw you can nag away. It's how the technology works, for every agent, on every job. So your agency doesn't lean on any one agent trusting itself — it builds the checking in as a separate step, done by someone other than whoever wrote the code.

Think of it like this: a contractor who's sure the wiring is right still gets it inspected. Confidence was never the test. A second, independent look is.

The checklist, written before the work starts

Every work order carries a checklist, written by the architect before a dev-lead touches any code. That order matters. Writing the checklist first means "done" was defined by the goal, not guessed backward from whatever got built.

A good checklist item is something a dev-lead can check on its own, with no screen to look at and no one in the room. "The button feels right" isn't checkable that way. "Running this command prints this result" is. That's why work orders lean on commands and tests, not impressions.

Tests: checks that run themselves

A test is a small program that checks another program. It runs a piece of code, compares what came out to what should have come out, and reports pass or fail — automatically, without a person watching. A dev-lead runs the tests its checklist names before calling a work order finished.

Tests catch a real class of mistake: code that looks right but breaks a case nobody thought to try by eye. They can't catch everything, though. A test only checks what it was written to check — which is one reason your agency doesn't stop at "the tests passed."

The validator's second look

Once every work order in a plan has landed, the validator — your agency's quality control — reviews the whole plan, one time. It doesn't take any dev-lead's word for it. It re-runs each work order's own checklist commands itself, then reads the actual result against what the work order promised.

The validator hands back one verdict per work order: verified, meaning the evidence backs up what was promised, or refuted, meaning it doesn't — with a plain reason why. Those verdicts go to the architect, the one who keeps track of the whole plan and decides what happens next.

When a work order stops to ask you something

Sometimes a dev-lead hits a fork it genuinely can't choose for you — the work order was unclear, or it found something that changes the plan. Rather than guess, it stops and marks the work order Needs review. That's a question, not a failure, and it waits for you in the Scheduler.

  1. Open the work order's card and read what it's asking. The Review button takes you straight to the Session that wrote it, where the question is waiting.
  2. Answer it there — clear up what was unclear, or point out what changed — then press Retry to send the work order back into the queue with your answer in hand.
  3. Or set it aside. If you leave it, your agency won't wait on you forever: it will eventually skip that one work order on its own, so anything else in the queue that isn't waiting on it can keep moving.
Watch out: "the tests pass" is not the same as "done." Tests only check what someone thought to write a test for. A work order can pass every test it names and still miss the point of what you actually asked for — that's exactly the gap the validator, and you, are there to catch.

You are the final judge

Checklists, tests, and the validator all exist to make your judgment easier, not to replace it. They filter out the obvious misses so what reaches you is worth your time. But whether the finished work actually solves your problem is still your call, not your agency's.

Look at what changed before you treat something as truly finished, especially for anything you care about getting right. A verified verdict is strong evidence. It isn't a substitute for you deciding the job is actually done.

What you learned

  • An agent can sound certain and still be wrong — checking is a separate step, done by someone else.
  • A work order's checklist is written before the work starts, so "done" means the goal, not whatever got built.
  • Tests are automatic checks a dev-lead runs itself, but they only catch what they were written to catch.
  • The validator reviews a whole plan once it's finished, re-running each checklist itself and handing back a verified or refuted verdict per work order.
  • Needs review is a question, not a failure — read it, fix and retry, or set it aside and let your agency move on. Either way, you're the one who decides a job is truly done.
