Meet your agency — the team that works for you

Session Manager isn't one assistant. It's a small agency of AI agents, and you're the client who hires them. This chapter introduces the team, what each member actually does, and why splitting the work up beats handing it all to one assistant.

Your own small agency

Picture a small creative agency. A client brings an idea. A project lead turns that idea into a plan and hands out pieces of it. Builders each take one piece and finish it. Someone else checks the finished work before it ships.

Session Manager runs the same way, except every role on the team is an AI agent. You describe what you want. One agent plans it. Other agents build the pieces. One more checks the result. You approve the plan, and you get the final say on whether the work is actually done.

Think of it like this: you're the client and the director of your own agency. You don't write the plan yourself, and you don't do the typing — you say what you want, and you sign off on what comes back.

The architect is the agent you actually talk to. It reads your project, asks a question when something's unclear, and turns a big idea into a series of small, well-defined work orders. It never writes the code itself — every work order it plans gets handed to a dev-lead instead. Because planning is worth taking slowly, the architect thinks with the most capable model available to it.

A dev-lead does exactly one work order, start to finish, with no view of the bigger plan. It reads the work order, makes the change, runs the checks it was given, and reports back. A dev-lead never decides what gets built or in what order — that call was already made by the architect before the work order reached it. Since a work order is already small and clearly scoped, a dev-lead runs on a faster, lighter model built for focused building rather than open planning.

The validator only appears once, at the end of a plan, after every one of its work orders has landed. It re-runs each one's own checks, compares the real result against what was promised, and gives a pass-or-fail verdict for each work order in the plan. It never edits your project and never adds new work orders of its own — it only reports what it found. Like a dev-lead, it runs on a fast model, because checking finished work is also a small, clear job.

A fourth agent, the Project Home builder, writes a single overview page for a project — what it is, how it's built, how to run it. It only runs when you ask for one; it never writes a page on its own.

How a job flows

Here's what happens between the moment you type a goal and the moment it's done:

  1. You describe what you want, in your own words.
  2. Architect plans the work and breaks it into small work orders.
  3. Work orders queued — each one waits its turn, ready to run.
  4. Dev-leads build them, one work order at a time, in the background.
  5. Validator checks every finished work order against what was promised.
  6. You review the result and decide what happens next.

You can keep working, or step away entirely, while the middle of that flow runs on its own. The work order queue and the Scheduler that runs it get their own chapter — this one is about who's doing the work, not how the calendar behind it works.

Why a team beats one assistant

A single assistant that plans, builds, and checks its own work has three problems a small team doesn't.

First, thinking is expensive, and typing shouldn't be. A careful plan is worth a slower, more capable model. Once that plan is broken into small, clear work orders, building each one doesn't need that same level of thought — a faster, lighter model finishes it just as well, for less. One assistant using its most capable setting for every step pays the planning price for typing work too.

Second, one assistant grading its own homework misses its own mistakes. The validator wasn't in the room while the work got built, so it checks with fresh eyes — the same reason a second person proofreads your writing better than you can.

Third, small tasks succeed more often than big ones. A work order with one clear goal is easy to check and easy to get right. A single sprawling task, done in one long pass, has more places to quietly go wrong — and no natural point to stop and check the result before moving on.

Your part in it

None of this runs without you. You still describe the goal, approve the plan before any work order runs, and read what comes back. The agency does the typing; you stay the director.

The four roles above are the ones Session Manager ships with, but they aren't the only agents you can have. You can write your own, with their own job description and their own rules, and add them to your team. Shaping your team covers how.

Try this: next time the architect hands you a plan, read the list of work orders before you approve it. That's the moment you're actually directing.

What you learned

  • Session Manager works like a small agency: you're the client and director, not the typist.
  • The architect plans and breaks work into small work orders — it never builds.
  • A dev-lead builds exactly one work order at a time, in the background, with no view of the bigger plan.
  • The validator checks a finished plan once, gives a pass-or-fail verdict per work order, and never edits your project.
  • A team beats one assistant: it saves thinking for planning, checks work with fresh eyes, and succeeds more often because each task stays small.
